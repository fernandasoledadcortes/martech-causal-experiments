Exercise 1 — Average Treatment Effect (ATE)

1.1 Experimental design validation

1.2 Average Treatment Effect (ATE) estimation

Two main models were estimated, one per required outcome. For conversion, a Linear Probability Model (LPM) was used: the coefficient is directly interpreted in percentage points. For revenue, OLS was applied to all users (including zeros). In both cases the raw estimator is consistent because randomization guarantees independence between treatment and potential outcomes. HC2 robust standard errors are reported.

Next step — Uplift modeling: an uplift modeling approach could be implemented to identify which user profiles were truly influenced by the countdown push, distinguishing them from those who would likely have converted even without treatment.

2.2 Marketing interpretation

  • Organic ($36.4): user with purchase intent, familiar with the brand. The countdown acts as the final nudge to close the sale.
  • Paid search ($18.9): has intent but is comparing alternatives. The effect exists but is moderate.
  • Social ($8.3): discovered the product in the feed, no prior intent. The countdown has marginal impact.

Implication: the countdown push is a closing tool, not a discovery tool. Its maximum value is with users who have already formed purchase intent.

Exercise 3 — Experimental Design: Checkout Flow Redesign

3.1 Experiment definition

DimensionDecisionJustification
Randomization unit User (user_id persistent), assigned at checkout start Randomizing by session violates SUTVA: the same user could see both versions. The user_id ensures consistent assignment across visits.

Assignment moment: at checkout start (not app open). The relevant denominator is users who reached checkout.

Unauthenticated users: use a persistent cookie as proxy.

App version: users on older versions may not render the new checkout. Filter by minimum version or report by version.
Assignment ratio 50% control / 50% treatment Maximizes statistical power for a given N.
Primary metric (OEC) Checkout conversion rate
= completed purchases / users who started checkout		 Direct indicator of checkout friction. High frequency and low variance vs. revenue. See section 3.1.1.
Guardrail metrics Revenue/user · time in checkout · abandonment rate · NPS Detect whether conversion is gained at the cost of experience or ticket size.
Minimum duration max(statistical days, 14 days) + optional burn-in period 14 days covers two complete Monday-Sunday cycles. The burn-in discards the learning effect of the new flow in the first days.

3.1.1 Primary metric selection

Candidate metricTypeAdvantageLimitation
✅ Checkout conversion rate
= purchases / users who started checkout		 Primary Directly tied to the goal; high frequency; low variance. Does not capture whether ticket size changes.
Revenue per userSecondary Captures the full effect (conversion × ticket). (1) Conceptual: at checkout start the user has already decided what to buy; the redesign affects whether they complete the purchase, not how much they spend. (2) Statistical: high variance → requires much larger N.
Cart abandonment rateSupplementary Identifies at which step the user drops off. Intermediate metric, not final.
Time to complete checkoutGuardrail Detects whether the new flow is slower. Does not measure whether the user buys.
Post-purchase NPS / satisfactionGuardrail Captures perceived experience. Lagged signal; high non-response rate.
Selection criterion: conversion rate is the dominant option. Revenue is reported as a secondary metric to validate that the effect translates to business outcomes.

3.2 Statistical power calculation

Empirical baseline: p₀ = 28.1%. An MDE of 2 pp means detecting an increase from 28.1% to 30.1%.

n = 2 × (zα/2 + zβ)² × p̅(1−p̅) / δ²

zα/2 = 1.9600  |  zβ = 0.8416  |  p̅ = 0.2912  |  δ = 2.00 pp

n = 8,100 per group (16,200 total)

With p₀ = 28.1% and MDE = 2 pp (α = 0.05, power = 0.80): 8,100 users per group (16,200 total). Consistent with power.prop.test() (n = 8,099).

3.2.2 Experiment duration

Two simultaneous constraints:

  • Statistical: days = ⌈ 16,200 users / daily_traffic ⌉.
  • Weekly / biweekly pay cycle: minimum 14 days to capture Monday-Sunday variation. In markets with biweekly pay (days 1 and 15, common in Mexico), 2 weeks guarantee at least one post-payday period and one pre-payday period — purchasing behavior differs between the two.

Recommended duration = max(statistical days, 14 days)

Note on the baseline: p₀ = 28.1% comes from the control group in Exercise 1 (push notifications). Ideally, the baseline should be calculated from historical checkout data (past month without interventions).

3.3 Threats to internal and external validity

ThreatType DescriptionMitigation
SRMInternal The treatment/control ratio deviates from the expected 50/50: assignment bug. Consequence: biased estimator and incorrect standard errors. Goodness-of-fit chi-square on observed vs. expected ratio: chisq.test(x = c(n_ctrl, n_trat), p = c(0.5, 0.5)). If p < 0.01, SRM confirmed — invalidate and review logs. Do not correct post-hoc.
Bug in the checkoutInternal The new checkout has errors. Direct consequences: revenue loss and unusable data. Three scenarios:

Critical — fails for a significant fraction of the treatment group. Action: pause immediately.

Minor — visual error without measurable impact on conversion. Action: continue and document the period.

Segment-specific — bug only on iOS 16 or version < 3.2. Action: exclude that segment and report separately.		 Operational monitoring from day 1 (distinct from statistical): alerts on HTTP error rate, load time, crashes, step-level abandonment. Does not read experiment results → does not break the statistical design.
PeekingInternal Reading statistical results before the pre-specified N inflates the false positive rate. With α = 0.05 and multiple looks it can exceed 30%. Pre-register the read date and N target.

No statistical peeking (conversion rate).
Monitor operational metrics (errors, crashes) — does not affect FPR.

Alternative: Sequential Testing (always-valid p-values).
Temporal heterogeneityExternal Experiment during atypical periods: Hot Sale (May), Buen Fin (November) or Black Friday raise the baseline conversion rate. The estimator does not generalize to the rest of the year. Maintain an e-commerce event calendar. If overlap occurs for operational reasons, stratify the analysis (special vs. normal period) and report the effects separately.

4.4 Limitations and assumptions

  • Selection into enrolled: clients with higher spending propensity tend to enroll more.
  • Parallel trends: formal test rejects the hypothesis (F=998.6, p<0.0001).
  • Conclusion: solid descriptive evidence of a positive association. For clean causal identification, an RCT or an eligibility discontinuity design would be required.
